# How Verbatim reads terminals

Status: accepted by Dickson on 2026-10-25, replacing the design of 2026-10-24, under five principles he agreed on 2026-10-24: one model of rows and one alignment; one read rule; a real reason for every rule; one flood policy, Core's; and an oracle that only judges. On 2026-10-25, after the remembered-history rule failed on repeated text, he decided that the reading may relax its requirements where that noticeably simplifies it: the memory holds only what the last read showed, with no inferred history and no inferred rotation; floods still respect the configured groups, but the count in "skipped N lines" need not be exact; once the history is full, a flood faster than a single read may say "skipped about N lines" and then its newest lines, its first lines lost; and the review cursor still moves reliably up through the whole buffer, exactly while the program is not writing, and by its line's text near its place while output arrives with a full history. Also on 2026-10-25 he decided the one exception to that memory, the aside screen (rule 8), kept to recognise a screen coming back and never spoken, and that a row of the last read's history block found by text is found only at its place or higher; and, after Claude Code's input box was drawn again lower (claude-code-reply recording), that a row the subsequence leaves gone is kept at the nearest new row with its text, whether or not that text is unique. Later on 2026-10-25 he decided that the design stops assuming no program writes into the history: a row of the history block found by text may be found lower by the one shift the screen's rows took when they went lower; a refused start that keeps nothing is a lost read unless the read's screen starts at the document's start or the aside screen is back; a program that erases the history and prints it again is heard only within the last read's reach, a listed limit; Enter's answer in a terminal is only new output, never the caret's line, as in NVDA; and a review command searches a screen below its place as well as above. Decisions keep their dates.

## The rules

Terms used in the rules:

- A row is one line of the terminal's grid. A line is the text between two line breaks; a long line wraps over several rows.
- A row's place is where it is in the terminal's buffer. Rows keep their places until the history is full; then each new line moves every row up one place. A program that erases the history and prints again (Pi's regular mode on Control+O and on a resize) gives the rows it prints new places, lower or higher.
- A read is one snapshot of the terminal's text, taken in one remote operation. The last read is the one the memory holds; its screen is the screen as that read found it, and its history block is the screen's worth of rows above that screen, which every read includes.
- The alignment pairs the last read's rows (the old rows) with the rows of a read. Each old row's outcome is kept (the same text, wherever it moved), grown (its text extended where it was), rewritten (paired with a new row like it) or gone. A new row paired with nothing is new.
- A key's window is the reads that begin within 300 milliseconds after the key reached the program.
- The quiet period is defined under "Volatile rows".

Each rule is followed by its reason: the real program, the measured terminal fact, or the NVDA capture or decision that needs it. "Recording" means a recording of that program in the conformance corpus; a name with a star, such as `*_typing`, is an end-to-end scenario run in both terminals.

### Reading rules

These decide what changed and what is said of it.

1. **When to read.** One read follows any number of Windows Terminal's text-changed and caret events and the console host's four console update WinEvents, with no delay; while Core's speech queue is full nothing is read until Core asks, at a group's last line, a caret event is only noted, and a held key is read before it is passed on and again after the terminal answers. Reason: measured: Windows Terminal moves its caret with no text change, and the console host's UIA text changes were lost in 2 of 14 large writes (2026-10-13); one console-host flood took 2,800 reads and 3,000 caret reads before these limits (Dickson, 2026-10-07 and 2026-10-14); NVDA's monitor; key holding (`docs/design/key-holding.md`).
2. **The read.** Each read is one remote operation from one screen above the top of the last read's screen to the document's end, with the screen, the caret and its line, starting at the provider range the last read placed by counting up from the document's end, never from the view; a refused start reads from one screen above the screen instead. Reason: measured: one `GetText` is atomic, the user can scroll the view, and Windows Terminal's clear shrinks the document, refusing the start.
3. **Rows.** A read's lines are cut into rows by the screen's width, and a row's padding, the spaces after its line's last character, is never part of it. Reason: measured: both terminals pad every row and give a wrapped line whole; Dickson, 2026-10-06.
4. **Positions.** When the read's rows above the last read's screen read exactly as the last read's history block did in the same places, and at least one of them has text, the last read's rows are where they were and every row below the end of the last read is new; otherwise the last read's rows are found by their text. Reason: measured: rows keep their places until the history is full and then move under fixed places; a program that erases the history and prints the same text at the same places leaves nothing a reader could tell, and one that prints other text there fails the test and is found by text (Pi's Control+O, pi-changed-above recording); blank rows a script printed to clear the screen read the same after the buffer moved; Dickson, 2026-10-25.
5. **The alignment.** The last read's rows are aligned with the read's so that the most are kept, in the fewest blocks, moving the fewest rows from their places; growth in place counts as kept, a row the subsequence leaves gone is kept at the nearest new row with its text, whether or not that text is unique (Dickson, 2026-10-25), leftover rows between kept rows (or a kept row and the read's edge) are paired as rewritten when they share half the longer row's characters in order, and everything attached to a row moves with it, except that a row rewritten alone starts its change history afresh; found by text, a row of the last read's history block is found at its place or higher, or lower by the one shift the last read's screen rows with text took when they went lower, and nowhere else (Dickson, 2026-10-25); rows above where the last read's first row went are history, silent, in every read from the kept start (Dickson, 2026-10-25); the caret's line is that line rewritten where it started, unless it only lost text while the caret moved forward over text both reads show unchanged (Dickson, 2026-10-25). Reason: a prompt paired with a copy of itself (the first build); Pi's fullscreen transcript repainted a row higher and its "Working..." row redrawn a row lower (recordings); Claude Code's slash-command list (recording); a gone row kept at the nearest copy of its text: Claude Code's input box, whose two equal borders are drawn again lower past the sent question, where the subsequence keeps one border and the other was spoken (claude-code-reply recording; Dickson, 2026-10-25); growth (NVDA capture in `*_long_lines`); "Model catalogs refreshed." (pi-regular recording; Dickson, 2026-10-24); the history block's rows found lower only at the screen's shift: the same long output printed twice in a full terminal, such as `type` of one file twice, puts the history's text lower while the screen's rows go up with the buffer, so the second copy stays new, and Pi's Control+O, which erases the history and prints it again longer, moves the screen's rows and the history down together, so its history is kept where it went and the rows above it, text the last read never covered, are silent (pi-changed-above recording; Dickson, 2026-10-25); the caret's line: Escape on a two-row line, and Windows PowerShell's `cls` in Windows Terminal, which homes the caret onto the first row and then writes the prompt there over a line that only lost text (`windows_terminal_typing`; Dickson, 2026-10-25).
6. **A lost read.** A read that keeps and pairs nothing of the last read and is no new screen is lost, positions trusted or not; so is a read whose positions are not trusted, whose start was not refused, and that keeps no row with text that the last read had at or above the caret, or keeps them equally well at more than one place. The whole document is then read once (with "report new output" off the memory starts again instead); when the aside screen is found in it, the read is a new screen, and otherwise it is aligned the same way: the rows below the last read's rows are new and counted as "about"; when the last read's rows are nowhere, every row is new, counted as "more than", or as "about" when the start of the read that asked for the whole document was refused, the document having shrunk under a clear; when they fit at more than one place, "skipped lines" is said with no number and only the visible screen is aligned. Reason: Dickson, 2026-10-25; Windows Terminal's clear followed by more than a screen of output before the next read, as `clear; dir` typed as one line (`windows_terminal_cleared_flood`; Dickson, 2026-10-25, floods needing no exact count); `*_scrollback_overflow`; `conhost_footer_overflow`, a footer below the caret kept while a flood passes it, which is why rows below the caret are left out of the test; a fresh console host's screen, and Windows Terminal's cleared one, starting at the document's start, so that the next read compares no rows and trusts positions while a write larger than the buffer moved every row (`conhost_fresh_flood`, `windows_terminal_clear_flood`; Dickson, 2026-10-25).
7. **Screens.** A read that keeps and pairs nothing of the last read is a new screen when its own screen starts at the document's start, or when the aside screen's rows with text are found in it at their places or higher; its visible rows are new and spoken, the rows above them are silent, and the review cursor goes to the caret. Reason: vim, less and Pi's fullscreen mode (recordings); Windows Terminal's clear, and `cls` saying the prompt (NVDA capture, `*_typing`); NVDA speaks the shell's screen when a pager closes (`nv-con-pager.txt`); with no history no text tells reliably when a full-screen program opens or closes, and Windows Terminal raises no event for it (measured); Dickson, 2026-10-24; a full-screen program closing over a long history (`*_pager_history`) against a flood larger than the history from a screen with no history, which no text of the last read tells apart (Dickson, 2026-10-25); a refused start alone does not make a new screen, since after Windows Terminal's clear with more than a screen of output every row is new, and a lost read's whole document tells that from a screen coming back (Dickson, 2026-10-25).
8. **The aside screen.** When a read is judged a new screen, the row hashes of the screen it replaced, its screen and history block, are kept aside, each with its distance from the document's end, replacing any earlier aside screen; a read that keeps something of the last read and whose own screen does not start at the document's start drops it. It is the one exception to the memory holding only what the last read showed: recognition only, never spoken, nothing inferred. Reason: vim, less, htop and Pi's fullscreen mode closing in a long-lived terminal, whose own reads keep their rows while their screen starts at the document's start (`*_pager_history`); Windows Terminal's clear followed by a command of more than the history (`windows_terminal_clear_flood`); Dickson, 2026-10-25.
9. **Lingering.** A row gone where it was, that a program had rewritten before or that was at or below the caret, is remembered for 2 seconds, and a new row with its text, the nearest first, is that row coming back. Reason: measured: the console host writes over a footer and draws it a row lower, and a read can land inside Ink's erase and redraw (Claude Code); no same-line condition (Dickson, 2026-10-24).
10. **A read with no text.** A read whose screen shows no text where the last read's had text changes nothing but the caret, and the next read is aligned against the memory as it was. Reason: measured: a read can land between a program's clear and its reprint; a script that clears by printing blank lines.
11. **Starting again.** A changed width or height, and a lost read with "report new output" off, start the memory again from the read with nothing aligned; each anchor is found again by its line's text nearest its old place, keeping its character within its line. Reason: Dickson, 2026-10-07 and 2026-10-24; measured: a resize rewraps every row; with output off a whole read would come every few hundred lines of a flood.
12. **New text.** New rows are output, their lines spoken in order, one utterance each, whole however long, through Core's flood policy; kept rows are silent wherever they moved; a blank line is never spoken but counts, and a blank row is a line only once a row with text is below it; a line or row that only lost text, or gained only white space, says nothing. Reason: NVDA and its diff; Dickson, 2026-10-06, 2026-10-07, 2026-10-08 and 2026-10-09; the first build's footer and marker-list scenarios; a list redrawn in two writes waits for its marker.
13. **Growing lines.** A line that grew speaks what it gained beyond what was said of it, without leading spaces, holding its unfinished last word (the caret at its end, no space after it) until the line grows past it, the caret leaves the line, or the quiet period passes. Reason: NVDA captures in `*_long_lines` and `*_full_screen`; the held word, Dickson, 2026-10-23, for streamed replies.
14. **Reprinted input.** The input line a shell reprints at the caret below new rows is not spoken again. Reason: readline and PSReadLine below a completion list (bash and psreadline recordings); Dickson, 2026-10-23.
15. **The focus.** Taking the focus subscribes and then reads, that read says only "terminal" and the caret's line ("blank" when empty), output written while the focus was elsewhere is never heard, and the memory lasts until the terminal closes. Reason: NVDA, including its capture of PowerShell's start-up notice; Dickson, 2026-10-09; `*_leave_flood`, `*_two_windows`, `windows_terminal_tabs`.
16. **Changed rows.** A rewritten row that is no key's answer, and did not only lose text, is silent when the one character that changed is neither a letter nor a digit (a spinner), held when volatile, and otherwise spoken from the start of the first word that changed, against what it last said. Reason: Pi's and Claude Code's spinners (progress recording; Dickson, 2026-10-07); vim's ruler and tig's count (Dickson, 2026-10-07 and 2026-10-08).
17. **Volatile rows.** A rewritten row that changed twice within 2 seconds, other than as a key's answer, or at two reads in a row of which the second waited for speech, is volatile: its first change is heard, and then it is silent until it has been still for the quiet period (1 second, or 1.5 times the time between its last two changes when that is longer, the reading's one timer), when it is spoken once. Reason: Dickson, 2026-10-23; once-a-second timers (the progress recording's "Elapsed", Pi, Claude Code); htop's meters; a build's progress bar during a flood (cargo); keys pausing for a second (fzf).
18. **Keys and their window.** Keys reach the rules through Core in the order they reached the program, each with that time; a key is credited with what reads in its window show, keys inside each other's windows share one window judged against the screen before the first, and a read's speech counts as after every key whose window holds it. Reason: Dickson, 2026-10-23 and 2026-10-24; NVDA's caret wait in Windows Terminal, 3 times 100 milliseconds; an echo taken for growth (first build); Up Arrow and a character at once, and "echo onex" cut off by its own key (`*_up_typing`).
19. **The caret's line.** After keys, when the caret ends on another line that was already there, that line is spoken whatever changed on it; when it stayed on its line, text the line gained that was not typed is spoken (typing in it included), text it lost next to the caret is spoken (Delete speaks what it leaves at the caret), and a move with no text change speaks the unit of a caret key Verbatim knows or the character or word the caret landed at; a caret move with no key is never spoken; Enter's answer is only new output, never the caret's line. Reason: NVDA, and its capture of "> banana" (`*_marker_list`); tab completion; NVDA captures "o" and "tw"; Dickson, 2026-10-08 and 2026-10-09; the editing keys of bash, PSReadLine and Pi (recordings); NVDA turns off its new-line announcement in every terminal, so Enter is passed on with no script and only inserted text is spoken (`docs/parity/terminals.md`, "Typing"), and Windows Terminal's `cls` from Windows PowerShell homes the caret onto the screen's first row in one write and erases in the next, a read between them finding the caret on a line that was already there (`windows_terminal_typing`; Dickson, 2026-10-25).
20. **Typing echo.** Typed characters are echoed when the terminal shows them and never spoken again as output, while output that is not the typing is output however short; a line changed some other way drops the held typing and speaks what it shows, typing the terminal cannot show yet is echoed when the caret passes over it, held typing is forgotten on Enter, Tab, Escape, Control+C, Control+D and Control+Break, and a character not shown within 300 milliseconds of reaching the program is dropped unspoken. Reason: NVDA, whose "probably typed" rule drops real output (`*_short_output`); Dickson, 2026-10-07, 2026-10-08, 2026-10-09 (Escape) and 2026-10-23 (a slow connection's echo); `*_typing`, `*_commands`, `*_spoken_password`.
21. **A key's answer.** A row away from the caret that, within a key's window, gained text (compared without spaces, its new text contains the old and adds more than at its end) or gained emphasis (of the rows whose colours changed while their text did not, the one whose colours now differ from both its neighbours') is the key's answer, spoken whole at once, unless it changed with no key in the 2 seconds before the key. Reason: tig, Microsoft Edit, Pi's "→" and Claude Code's list (recordings), `*_redrawn_list` (Dickson, 2026-10-23); htop, tig and PSReadLine's MenuComplete show the selection by colour alone; htop's meters refresh on their own every 1.5 seconds.
22. **Held for keys.** Every other row the keys changed is held while keys keep coming and spoken once, by its changed words, when no key has been pressed for the quiet period, unless it only lost text. Reason: fzf's count, vim's ruler and relative line numbers (Dickson, 2026-10-23); the lost-text silence keeps a list's old selected row silent when its marker moves (Dickson, 2026-10-24).
23. **Suggestions.** Text after the caret on its row, in colours other than the typing's and not typed, is a suggestion, spoken when it appears and when it becomes other than the last suggestion less what was typed. Reason: PSReadLine's prediction, fish and zsh; Dickson, 2026-10-07 and 2026-10-23.
24. **Colours.** The whole screen's colours are read with the text in the first read after each key, the key's echo waits for that read, and colours are read at no other time. Reason: Dickson, 2026-10-24; the measured costs under "Costs".
25. **The review anchor.** The review position is a place in the buffer, the hash of its line and a column: within the last read it moves with its row's outcome and follows the caret only with "review follows caret" on when a key moved the caret within its window or the focus arrived; above the last read, a review command reads by place, in one remote operation, the rows from one screen above its place to the document's end and takes the nearest row with its line's text from a screen above its place to a screen below it; when no row has that text, it keeps its place and reads the row there now; with the read's start refused, an anchor above the read is found by its line's text within the read, or goes to the caret. Reason: Dickson, 2026-10-23 and 2026-10-25; `*_review_output`; measured: before the history is full, and while nothing is written, the place is exact, a full history moves rows up under fixed places, and a program printing its history again longer moves them down (Pi's Control+O, which moved every row 17 lower); a refused start means the document shrank beneath the rows above the read (Pi's second Control+O, pi-changed-above recording; Dickson, 2026-10-25).

### Features and settings

These are not reading rules: they decide how what the reading rules found is spoken, or offer commands.

- **Core's flood policy**, the only one; the reading rules hand it every new line. The first "Lines spoken in full" (30) lines of a burst are spoken whole, and once heard, if more than that wait, all but the newest "Last lines to speak" (30) become "skipped N lines", repeated for each group, either limit settable up to 10,000; every line counts, blank lines included, a line and its growth counting and dropping as one. A count the reading took from text after a lost read says "skipped about N lines"; lines that went by past a full history with nothing of the last read found make "skipped more than N lines", N the rows less the lines about to be spoken; and a lost read whose rows fit at more than one place says "skipped lines" with no count. A key other than Shift cuts speech and drops waiting output, output read before the cut is never spoken after it while output read after it is, and a cut takes the read owed at that moment, if any, using its output only to echo typing and answer keys; Shift pauses and resumes. Waiting output is bounded at 10 MB, a larger single line cut on a grapheme boundary with "line cut"; Verbatim+5 turns "report new output" off and on, saying which, and off drops waiting output. Reason: Dickson, 2026-10-06, 2026-10-07, 2026-10-09, 2026-10-10, 2026-10-14, 2026-10-17 and 2026-10-25 ("about"); NVDA; the flood, history-flood, scrollback-overflow, control-flood and shift-flood scenarios, and NVDA's captures in `*_flood` and `*_full_screen`.
- **Speak passwords.** With it on, typing is echoed as it is typed. Reason: NVDA; `*_spoken_password`.
- **Keyboard layout.** The console host's keys are translated with its own keyboard layout. Reason: NVDA.
- **Notifications.** Every UIA notification from a terminal is ignored. Reason: NVDA's overlays block them; Dickson, 2026-10-06.
- **Text as it is.** Lines are spoken as they are, symbols included, in one language for the terminal. Reason: Dickson, 2026-10-07 and 2026-10-08, until symbol processing (M8).
- **Review line ends.** A review line ends at its last character. Reason: Dickson, 2026-10-06; `*_review_padding`.
- **Moving by command.** Enter on a line a key changed sets a mark on that line once the terminal answers, and Verbatim+Alt+Up Arrow and Verbatim+Alt+Down Arrow move the review cursor to the previous and next mark. Reason: Dickson, 2026-10-23; Windows Terminal's `autoMarkPrompts` uses the same signal.
- **Report status bar.** NVDA's "report status bar" gestures read the footer: the rows below the caret, or the rows at the bottom of the screen a program has rewritten, or with neither the last row with text. Reason: Dickson, 2026-10-23.

That is 25 reading rules and 8 features. Gone from the design of 2026-10-24: the remembered history, the rotation and its candidates, the first-row tie-break, the history match of whole reads, set-aside screens other than the one aside screen of rule 8, which is never returned to, and the overflow rules.

## Where it runs

Decided by Dickson on 2026-10-24 and not reopened here: the memory, the alignment and the rules run in Core, in the reducer, beside the rest of what Verbatim decides. The terminal's outpost makes the accessibility calls only: it reads what Core asks for and sends the text back. Keys reach the rules from the keyboard hook through Core, in the order they reached the program. So there is one caret, Core's, and one record of keys, Core's; a caret any read or caret event reports is the caret the rules judge the next key against.

What stays in the outpost: the provider range where the next read starts, the screen's last height and width, the merging of change signals and the reading-on-demand state machine (so a flood does not wake Core per line), and the noted caret.

## Evidence

The facts the rules rest on. "Measured" means the measurements of 2026-10-06 to 2026-10-24 recorded in `docs/performance.md` and the conformance runs; NVDA's files are under `nvda/source/` at the pinned commit.

### The terminals

- One `GetText` is atomic with respect to the terminal's writes, not the program's: a line the program writes in two calls, or ConPTY delivers in two chunks, can end a read half written. Calls of one remote operation run together in the terminal's process. The terminal's writer waits while a read copies.
- The document ends with the lower of the last row with text and the caret's row: rows an insertion moved down stay in it, and a row erased at the end leaves it.
- Rows keep their places until the history is full; then each new line moves the whole buffer up one place under fixed places, so a kept range reads newer text.
- Both terminals pad every row to the width and give a wrapped line whole. The console host's ranges end before a row's line break; Windows Terminal's end after it, except on the buffer's last row, which ends one cell short.
- The console host keeps 9,001 rows in all, Windows Terminal 9,001 rows of history above its screen, by default.
- The alternate screen is one screen tall, with no history. A range kept on the main screen reads the alternate screen silently when its row is within the alternate screen's height, and is refused beyond it.
- The console host's `cls` pushes the screen into the history; Windows Terminal's clear erases the screen and the history, so the document shrinks. Windows PowerShell 5.1's `cls` in Windows Terminal homes the caret in one write and erases in the next, and the caret event between them lets a read find the caret on the old first row with the text unchanged (`windows_terminal_typing`'s flight recorder, 2026-10-25). Control+L in PSReadLine under Windows PowerShell 5.1 and in Git for Windows' bash 5.3 said only the prompt in each terminal, 20 presses a shell in Windows Terminal and 12 in the console host: no read landed between a homed caret and the erase (probe of 2026-10-25; PowerShell 7 not installed).
- A refused start means exactly that the document is shorter than at the last read: a clear with `ESC[3J`, a screen switch, or a program printing its history again shorter. Found by text, the last read's screen rows found lower than their places are the one cheap sign of a history printed again longer, which an accepted start does not reveal (pi-changed-above recording, reads 20 and 21, both terminals).
- A scroll region from the screen's top with a footer below it scrolls into the history in both terminals. The console host was seen writing a line over the footer's row and drawing the footer again a row lower.
- Reverse video covers only the cells written in it, so a selected row reads as several colours; telling a selected row needs each row's colour runs. A stretch of cells answers its first cell's colour when every cell but its last has it, and "mixed" otherwise; Windows Terminal does not implement `FindAttribute`.
- Windows Terminal serves UI Automation on its UI thread; with software rendering, a call that arrives during a heavy frame waits for it (10 to 36 milliseconds), while a read made on the text-changed event, which comes once the frame is done, did not wait. It raises no text-changed event for writes made only of blank lines.
- The console host's four console WinEvents are its change signals: a region update for each write of a row's cells, a simple update for one cell (each typed character's echo) and for a whole-buffer clear, a scroll update for text scrolled up, and a layout event for the view moving, a resize and the alternate screen opening or closing. UIA's text changes stopped reaching Verbatim in the middle of 12,000-line writes in 2 of 14 runs, so since 2026-10-13 the WinEvents are the only signal taken there (`docs/design/wake-ups.md`, item 15).
- The console host's scroll update counts rows the text moved up in its window, not rotations of the buffer (measured 2026-10-25 on build 26200, a probe outside Verbatim with a 40-microsecond callback, `docs/performance.md`, "A full-screen program's exit"). Once the buffer is full each line raises one with a vertical distance of minus one, and their sum equalled the rotation exactly for 1, 1,000 and 20,000 lines, none lost; but the same minus one comes for each line the view scrolls down before the buffer is full (8,950 of them while 9,000 lines filled it, with no rotation) and for each line a scroll region scrolls, `ESC[2J` gives one event of the rows it pushed into the history, `ESC[3J` one of plus the history's rows, and `ESC[S`, `ESC[L`, `ESC[M` and the alternate screen give none. The reading does not use it (Dickson, 2026-10-25).
- Every write the same probe made raised at least one console update: a write on the bottom row with no line feed a region update, a write that wraps from the bottom row a region update and a scroll update, `ESC[S`, `ESC[L` and `ESC[M` each a region update.
- A range kept at the alternate screen's top is accepted on the main screen after the alternate screen closes, in both terminals, and lies above where the next read would start (measured 2026-10-25).
- Windows Terminal's notifications carry printed characters with no positions, and the console host's will report only the span the cursor travelled, so neither tells new output from a redraw. The events are signals to read, not content (decided).
- Only the console host tells its buffer's size through UI Automation; the text pattern says nothing of rows past the document's end in either.

### NVDA

- It reads the whole document on every change, diffs it by character, speaks only inserted text, and has no flood limit. On 2026-10-06 the document after 5,000 lines in a 120-column Windows Terminal was 610,244 characters and took 6 milliseconds to read before any diff.
- Monitoring starts on the focus with a baseline read; output written just after the focus is spoken (captured: "terminal blank" and then PowerShell's start-up notice).
- Events are coalesced: UIA's own coalescing, NVDA's rate limiter, and a monitor flag that turns any number of events between two reads into one more read.
- It hides typed text with "probably typed" rules that also drop real short output, holds typed characters until any text change, and forgets them on Enter, Tab, Control+C, Control+D and Control+Pause.
- Its wait for the caret after a caret key is 100 milliseconds, times 3 in Windows Terminal; Backspace speaks what was before the caret only if the caret moved within the wait, and Delete speaks the unit at the caret.
- Its cancel shares the queue with its line reports, so lines queued before a key are cut off and lines from a read that ends after the key are still spoken.
- It speaks nothing for keys it has no script for, has nothing for full-screen programs, footers, lists or moving by command, and its review cursor is a live range that follows every caret event.
- Its captured weaknesses: fragments from the character diff, typed commands and recalled lines spoken twice, queued password characters spoken on another change, and no flood control.

### The programs

- Shells (readline, PSReadLine) edit the input line in place, move the caret without writing, print completion lists below the prompt and reprint the input below them, and reprint the input below output that arrives while the user types. PSReadLine without its screen-reader mode rewrites the input line on every key to recolour it. Its prediction, and fish's and zsh's suggestions, write grey text after the caret.
- Ink programs (Claude Code) write finished output above a region they erase and rewrite each frame, up to 30 a second.
- Pi 1.4.0: fullscreen mode on the alternate screen, repainting its transcript to scroll and printing its last frame on the main screen as it leaves; regular mode on the main screen, rewriting its bottom rows a row lower for each streamed line, and clearing the screen and the history and reprinting everything on a resize or a change above the screen, as Control+O expanding or collapsing tool output (117 rows to 136 and back to 119 in the pi-changed-above recording). Its cursor is hidden and parked in its editor after each frame; its spinner turns every 80 milliseconds and its "Elapsed" line ticks every second; a tool's state is shown by background colour.
- Full-screen programs (vim, less, htop, fzf) put the caret anywhere, keep status lines at the top or bottom, and scroll by rewriting rows or by scroll regions; htop refreshes its meters every 1.5 seconds by default.
- Progress: one line rewritten after a carriage return, or several rows rewritten in place above the caret (docker pull, npm), or a bar on the last row below the lines a build prints (cargo).

## The model

The screen is rows. Every operation a program can perform (write, line feed, carriage return, cursor moves, erase, insert or delete rows and characters, scroll regions, switching screens, resizing, colour changes) leaves each old row kept, rewritten or gone, and each new row an old row or new. So an alignment of old rows to new rows describes any change completely, and the alignment that keeps the most rows, moved in the fewest blocks, is the explanation that assumes the program did the least. That one alignment decides what is new, where each old row went, where the review cursor goes, and what a key did. Which operations happened between two reads cannot be observed; where two explanations fit every read, the rules take the one the alignment prefers, and the cases where that can be wrong are listed under "Limits". A mistake lasts for one read: the next read is aligned against what this read showed, and nothing older is kept.

### The memory

For each terminal, kept in Core while the terminal exists, across focus changes:

- The last read's rows, its history block and its screen: each row's text without padding, a 64-bit hash of it, whether it continues onto the next row, its colour runs when last read, whether a program has rewritten it, and its history: what it last said, when it last changed and the change before, and whether it is volatile.
- Lingering rows, with their history, for 2 seconds.
- Anchors: the review position and the command marks, each a place in the buffer, a column, and the hash of its line.
- What waits for the quiet period: unfinished words, volatile rows, rows held for keys, marks waiting for their line.
- The aside screen, while one is held ("The aside screen"): the hashes of the rows with text of the screen the last new screen replaced, each with its distance from the document's end, about a kilobyte.

Nothing older than the last read is kept but the aside screen: about the last read's rows, a few kilobytes per terminal.

## The read

A read is one remote operation that returns:

- the text from one screen above the top of the last read's screen to the document's end, lines whole and padding kept;
- the screen's text, for its width and height;
- the caret's row and column and its line;
- a provider range at the top of this read's screen, placed by counting up from the document's end by the screen's height, which the outpost keeps as the next read's start;
- whether the start was refused, and whether the read reached the document's start;
- after a key, the screen's colour runs (see "Colours").

The read starts a screen above the top of the last read's screen so that the read holds the last read's history block, which "Positions" compares, and so that a full history that moved up by less than two screens still holds the last read's screen inside the read. When the start is refused (Windows Terminal's clear shrank the document below it), the read starts a screen above the screen instead. A terminal's first read knows no screen's height and takes only the screen; when the document goes on above it, it is read again at once, counted up from the document's end by the height it found, with the screen's worth of rows above, so that it holds its history block too (Claude, 2026-10-25, pending Dickson's acceptance). The whole document is read only for a lost read ("A lost read"), at most once per group of spoken output since reads wait while Core's speech queue is full, and never with "report new output" off.

A user scrolling the view does not move the read: its start was placed from the document's end, so scrolling neither moves it nor makes each read as long as the distance scrolled.

## The rules in detail

### Rows and the width

Lines are split into rows by the width and rebuilt from rows for speech; a read's first row may continue a line from above the read, and that line keeps its start. The width is the length of the caret's row, which the read gives as one row (one cell more on the row Windows Terminal cuts short), when that length fits every line on the screen; when two lengths fit, the one that gives the screen the height it had; otherwise the narrowest line on the screen but its last, which is wrong only when every one of those lines wraps. Padding is only what follows a line's last character, so spaces at the end of a wrapped line's earlier rows, and spaces a program writes inside a line (Pi aligning its model's name to the right), are text; a row wrapping onto rows with no text keeps no spaces.

### Positions

Each read starts at the place where the last read's screen started, less a screen. Until the history is full, rows keep their places, so the read's rows above the last read's screen read exactly as the last read's history block did, unless a program erased the history and printed other text there, which fails the test and is found by text. When they do, and at least one of them has text, the last read's rows are where they were: the alignment keeps them at their places, and every row below the end of the last read is new, counted exactly by place. Once the history is full, every new line moves every row up one place under fixed places, and the rows read above the last read's screen hold newer text; then the last read's rows are found by their text. The text condition matters after a script clears by printing blank lines: blank rows read the same after the buffer moved.

A read whose start is the document's start (the alternate screen, or a main screen with no history yet) compares the rows it has; with none above the last read's screen, positions are trusted while the document did not shrink, since with no history the history cannot be full and nothing moved (Claude, 2026-10-25, pending Dickson's acceptance). A single write of more than the buffer holds moves every row even so; such a read keeps nothing of the last read, so "Screens" and "A lost read" judge it. Only rows both reads hold are compared. A terminal's first read holds its history block like every later read ("The read"), so the next read's rows above its screen are compared, and when the focus returns to a terminal the outpost reads afresh, rows written while it was away are history found in place (`windows_terminal_leave_flood`), while more than two screens of output before the next read move the buffer under them and make a lost read.

### The alignment

The alignment is a longest common subsequence of row hashes. Among alignments that keep equally many rows, the one whose kept rows move in the fewest blocks wins (a block is a run of kept rows moved by the same number of rows), and then the one that moves the fewest rows from their places. So a repeated row (a prompt, a blank, a rule line) is never paired with a copy of itself elsewhere, a program's bottom rows are kept where they stay, and a transcript repainted a row higher is one block. A subsequence keeps rows in order, so a screen cleared into the history over repeated text pairs each copy with the copy in the same place (Dickson, 2026-10-24). A row the subsequence leaves gone is kept at the nearest new row with its text, whether or not that text is unique, which a subsequence alone cannot do when it moved past others: a list entry moved past the others, or Claude Code's input box drawn again lower past the question just sent, whose two borders read the same and only one of which the subsequence can keep (claude-code-reply recording; Dickson, 2026-10-25). A row of the last read's history block is not looked for this way, so it is still found only where the subsequence may find it. A row whose new text starts with its old text, at its place, has grown, and counts as kept. With positions trusted, the old rows' places are known and the alignment keeps rows where they were; found by text, the places a block may move to are free, except that a row of the last read's history block is found at its place or higher, or lower by the one shift the last read's screen rows with text took when they went lower, the commonest shift of a vote over their hashes, and nowhere else (Dickson, 2026-10-25): the same long output printed twice in a full terminal, such as `type` of one file twice, puts the history's text lower while the screen's rows go up with the buffer, so it is not taken for the old copy's history rows and silenced, while a program that erases the history and prints it again longer, as Pi's regular mode on Control+O, moves the screen's rows down with the history, which is then kept where it went. Its place is where the read's start puts it, or, with the start refused, where the document's start puts it when both reads began there; otherwise it is not known and nothing limits it. Rows of the screen are not limited, since a footer moves down. In every read from the kept start, accepted or refused, the rows above where the last read's first row went are history, silent: under a rotation nothing is above it, and after a history printed again longer they are text the last read never covered. Found by text from an accepted start, the first row went where the history block's kept rows went, and when none was kept nothing is taken for history.

Between two kept rows, or a kept row and the read's edge, the old and new rows left over are paired as rewritten when two rows share at least half the characters of the longer, in order; among such pairings the one that moves rows by the same amount as the kept row beside them wins. So Pi's status row "⠋ Working..." that moved down a row, as a streamed line was written above it, pairs with "⠙ Working...", and the streamed line is new. The caret's line is identified by its first row: when the caret's line now starts where the last read's caret line, which had text, started, and that row is otherwise new, it is that line rewritten (Escape on a two-row line), unless the newer line only lost text, its whole text compared without spaces so a wrapped line counts all its rows, while the caret moved forward along it over text both reads show unchanged in the same columns, counted in characters along the line as the reading rules count the caret (Dickson, 2026-10-25). Then a program wrote another line where it was: Windows PowerShell's `cls` in Windows Terminal homes the caret onto the first row in one write and erases in the next, so a read between them leaves the caret at the first row's start, and the prompt the erase leaves there, "ready>" in place of "ready> .\secret.ps1", is not that line but a new screen's, and is said (`windows_terminal_typing`). A space typed to dismiss a grey suggestion also leaves a line that only lost text with the caret moved forward, but over a cell whose text changed, the suggestion's next character now a space, so it stays that line; PSReadLine's MenuComplete restored by Escape moved the caret back, and says what it removed.

The alignment maps every old row to its outcome, and everything attached to the row moves with it: the review position and the marks, the caret before a key, what the row last said, its change times and volatility, its colours. A row rewritten alone, paired with a row elsewhere with no neighbour kept in the same move, keeps what it last said but starts its change history afresh (Dickson, 2026-10-24), because in Pi's regular mode the line "Model catalogs refreshed." pairs with a row that had changed before and would otherwise be held as volatile and never spoken (pi-regular recording).

### A lost read

A read that keeps and pairs nothing of the last read and is no new screen ("Screens") is lost, whether positions are trusted or not (Dickson, 2026-10-25): a fresh console host's screen, or Windows Terminal's cleared one, starts at the document's start, so the read after it compares no rows and trusts positions, and a single write of more than the buffer holds still moves every row (`conhost_fresh_flood`, `windows_terminal_clear_flood`). Otherwise a read is lost when positions are not trusted, its start was not refused, and it keeps no row with text that the last read had at or above the caret, or keeps them equally well at more than one place. Rows below the caret are left out of that test because a footer drawn below a scroll region stays where it is while a flood scrolls past it (`conhost_footer_overflow`); if the footer counted, a flood larger than two screens would never be seen as lost.

The whole document is then read once. When the aside screen's rows are found in it at their places or higher, the read is a new screen ("The aside screen"); otherwise it is aligned the same way (Dickson, 2026-10-25):

- When the last read's rows are found in it at one place, the rows below them are new, and the flood policy counts what it skips of them as "skipped about N lines": the count is taken from text, so repeated text can make it wrong by a little. In a full terminal a 2,000-line flood says its first 30 lines, "skipped about 1941 lines", the newest 29 and the prompt.
- When the last read's rows are nowhere, every row is new, and what is skipped is "skipped more than N lines", N the document's rows less those about to be spoken (`*_scrollback_overflow`); but when the start of the read that asked for the whole document was refused, the document shrank under a clear and everything in it is new: "skipped about N lines" (Dickson, 2026-10-25). After `clear` and a hundred lines before the next read in a terminal with some history, Windows Terminal's reading says the burst's first lines, "skipped about 71 lines", the last 29 and the prompt (`windows_terminal_cleared_flood`).
- When they fit at more than one place, as after a flood of identical lines, "skipped lines" is said with no number, and only the visible screen is aligned against the last read: a flood of 2,000 "the same line" in a full terminal says the line 30 times, "skipped lines", and the prompt.

With "report new output" off nothing waits, so a whole read would follow every few hundred lines of a flood; it is not taken, and the memory starts again from the read ("Starting again"). What an overflow pushes out while held unspoken, an unfinished word or a volatile row's latest value, is not heard (Dickson, 2026-10-24).

### Lingering

A row a read finds gone where it was (erased or written over, not moved away by a scroll) that a program had rewritten before, or that was at or below the caret before or after the read, is kept for 2 seconds with its history. A new row with its text, the nearest first, takes it back, history and all (no same-line condition: Dickson, 2026-10-24), so a paragraph an Ink region regrows and rewraps after a read caught it erased is not spoken again. Rows that leave by scrolling never linger, so a pager scrolled back a line speaks the line it brings back (`*_full_screen`, `*_pager`). The 2 seconds are the same limit that defines a row that keeps changing; every redraw seen, Ink's at 30 frames a second and the console host's footer, completes within one frame.

### A read with no text

A read whose screen shows no text where the last read's screen had text is taken for what it most likely is, a read landing between a program's clear and its reprint: the memory stays as it was, only the caret is taken from the read, and the next read is aligned against the memory. So a reprint is silent where it repeats what was there, and a script that clears by printing blank lines is judged at the first read with text. A screen that is still blank at the next read is read the same way.

### Screens

A read that keeps no row with text of the last read and pairs none as rewritten, rows below the caret included, is a new screen when its own screen starts at the document's start, or when the aside screen is found in it ("The aside screen"). Its visible rows are new and spoken; the rows above them are silent; the review position goes to the caret, since nothing it was on survived; and the command marks wait for their lines, found again by their line's text when a later read shows it, so marks set in the shell survive a full-screen program. Nothing is returned to (Dickson, 2026-10-24): the screen a new screen replaced is kept only to recognise it coming back: opening vim or less speaks its screen, paging speaks each page, and closing it speaks the shell's screen as it comes back, at most a screen, through the flood policy, cut by any key, as NVDA does when a pager closes (`nv-con-pager.txt`); Pi's last frame printed on the main screen as it leaves is part of that screen. Windows Terminal's clear leaves the prompt as the only row of a new screen, its screen at the document's start, so `cls` says the prompt. A refused start with neither, as Windows Terminal's clear followed by more than a screen of output before the next read, is a lost read, whose whole document tells a clear from a screen coming back (Dickson, 2026-10-25). This replaces the decision of 2026-10-09 that closing a full-screen program says only what changed: with no history, no text tells reliably when a full-screen program opens or closes, and Windows Terminal raises no event for it.

A read that keeps nothing in any other case is a lost read.

### The aside screen

When a read is judged a new screen, the screen it replaced, the last read's screen and history block, is set aside: for each of its rows with text, the hash and its distance from the document's end at that read. It stays until another new screen replaces it, or until a read keeps something of the last read while its own screen does not start at the document's start, which drops it (Dickson, 2026-10-25). A later read that keeps and pairs nothing of the last read finds it when a row with text of it is in the read no nearer the document's end than it was, which is its place or higher, since rows only move up and the document's end only down: that read is the earlier screen coming back, and a new screen. A lost read's whole document is searched for it too. Distances are counted from the document's end as the read's own start is placed; they would mislead only if the document's end moved up between the two reads, which a program closing does not do, since the shell prints its prompt where it was or below.

So vim, less, htop or Pi's fullscreen mode closing over a long history, with the buffer full or not and with the shell printing lines after it, is a new screen: the aside rows are found at their places, or higher once the buffer is full. The program's own reads keep its rows, but its screen starts at the document's start, so they do not drop the aside screen. Windows Terminal's clear sets aside the screen before it, whose rows the clear erased, so a command of more than the history after it is a lost read, "skipped more than N lines", like one after a fresh console host's first screen, which has no aside screen. Back in a shell with history, the first read that keeps a row drops it, so a screen a program leaves behind cannot later take a flood for its return.

It is the one exception to the memory holding only what the last read showed (Dickson, 2026-10-25): about a kilobyte of hashes, for recognition only, never spoken, with nothing inferred from it.

### Starting again

Two cases start the memory again from the read: a changed width or height, and a lost read with output reporting off. Every row's history begins afresh and nothing is aligned. After a resize only the caret's line is spoken, if it changed; with output off nothing is. Each anchor is looked for by its line's hash, the nearest occurrence to its old place, keeping its character position within the line, so after a resize the review cursor stays on the same character however the line now wraps (Dickson, 2026-10-24); the review position not found goes to the caret, and a mark not found waits for its line.

### New output

New rows are output, joined into lines where they wrap, and handed in order to Core's flood policy. A line seen for the first time is spoken whole at once, so a prompt that ends without a space is heard without waiting. A line that grew speaks what it gained beyond what was said of it: what was said of a line is remembered with its rows, and a line counted in a flood's summary counts as said, so a line that lost text and later gained it back says only the new part, and spaces gained alone wait for the word after them. Once a line has grown, its last word is held while it is unfinished (the caret at its end, no space after it) and spoken when the line grows past it, when the caret leaves the line (including when the line scrolls away), or after the quiet period.

A new blank row is a line once a row with text is written below it; blank rows the caret rests after, with nothing below, are not lines yet. A line feed makes a line; a cursor move does not.

The input reprinted: when after a read the caret is on a different row whose line equals the caret's line as last read, with any characters typed since, and that line held input (a key changed it since it appeared) and new rows lie between the two, the shell has reprinted the input below a completion list or below output that arrived while the user typed, and that row is not spoken; characters typed since are echoed as usual. A fresh prompt after Enter is not this, and is spoken.

### Changed rows

A rewritten row is judged in this order: a key's answer (see "Lists and colours"); lost text or white space only, silent; held for keys; a spinner, silent; volatile, silent and held; otherwise its changed words are spoken, from the start of the first word where it differs from what it last said (Unicode word rules, whole graphemes). A row is volatile when it changed at two reads within 2 seconds, other than as a key's answer, or at two reads in a row of which the second had waited for Core's speech queue, when the time between them says nothing about how often the row changes. So the first change of a timer is heard ("Working... 1s"), the user knows the row exists, and "Working... 34s" is heard once the work ends; a clock that never stops is heard once, at its first tick. A volatile row stops being volatile once it has been spoken after settling. A change already waiting in Core's queue is not replaced by the row's next change (Dickson, 2026-10-24); a row that changes faster than the queue drains is volatile and held instead.

### Keys

Keys reach the rules through Core with the time each reached the program, taken on the clock every process of a run shares (`crates/verbatim-process/src/clock.rs`). Each read request carries the last key Core knew of when it asked, and each read its start time, so a read is judged against every key that reached the program before it began, never by when a message arrived. A read's speech counts as after the newest key whose window holds it, so the cut a later key makes does not drop it: a key deferred behind a held key does not silence the held key's answer.

A key's effects are what reads beginning within its window show; later changes are output. Keys that arrive within each other's windows share one window, and the screen before the first is what they are judged against: Up Arrow followed at once by "x" speaks the recalled line with the "x" in it once, "echo onex", without the character's own echo (Dickson, 2026-10-09).

The caret's line is identified by its first row, so a key that empties a wrapped line's second row (Escape on a two-row line) is judged on that line. After the keys, the caret's line before them is followed through the alignment, and:

- when the newest key is Enter, the caret's line is not spoken, whatever happened to it: Enter's answer is only new output, as NVDA turns off its new-line announcement in terminals (Dickson, 2026-10-25), so a read between Windows Terminal's two writes of `cls`, which homes the caret onto the screen's first row before it erases, says nothing, and the prompt the erase then writes there is a new screen's and is said ("The alignment");
- when the caret now rests on another line that was already there, that line is spoken whatever changed on it: a list whose caret follows its ">" marker says "> banana" (NVDA capture, `*_marker_list`), and Up Arrow in a pager says the line it moved to;
- when the caret rests on a new line, that line is output, spoken by "New text";
- when the caret stayed on its line and the line gained text the user did not type (a tab completion, Control+R's match, a paste, a recalled line), the gain is spoken, typing in it included;
- when it stayed and the line lost text next to the caret, what it lost is spoken, whether the program erased it in one write or over several reads; Delete instead speaks what it leaves at the caret;
- when it stayed and the line did not change but the caret moved along it, a caret key Verbatim knows speaks its unit, and any other key the landing rule: one character along, that character; at the start or end of the line or a word, the character or word there. A line a read found gone and that came back counts as changed. A caret that a program moved while it drew and parked back where the keys found it did not move.

Typed characters follow `docs/parity/terminals.md`: echoed when the terminal shows them; never spoken again as output; a line changed some other way, such as a password prompt's asterisks, drops the held typing and speaks what it shows; a clock on a password prompt's line never speaks the password; typing the terminal cannot show yet (a space at a line's end, a character typed over a suggestion that showed it already) is echoed when the caret moves over it; typing past the right margin is echoed, not spoken as a new line. A character not shown within 300 milliseconds of reaching the program is dropped unspoken, judged at the next read with no timer.

### Lists and colours

One rule for every key, with no knowledge of list markers. A row away from the caret that gained text within the key's window is the key's answer and is spoken whole at once: compared without spaces, its new text contains the old and adds more than at its end (a ">" or "→" before an entry, "[x]" for "[ ]"). A row that gained emphasis is the answer too: of the rows whose colours changed while their text did not, the one whose colours now differ from both its neighbours'. A row that changed with no key in the 2 seconds before the key, such as a meter, is not its answer. Every other row the keys changed (fzf's count, vim's ruler) is held while keys keep coming and spoken once, by its changed words, when no key has been pressed for the quiet period; a row that only lost text stays silent, as everywhere, so a list's old selected row is silent when its marker moves, and fzf's count is heard when it grows but not when it shrinks (Dickson, 2026-10-24). So when a list is filtered as the user types, the entries that survive are kept rows and silent, the row that gained the pointer is spoken after the echo, and the count once typing pauses.

On the caret's row, text after the caret in colours other than the typed text's, which the user did not type, is a suggestion: spoken when it first appears and when it becomes something other than the previous suggestion less the character typed, so typing along it does not repeat it; accepting it (Right Arrow, End) speaks the key's unit.

Colours are read for the whole screen in the first read after each key, in the same remote operation as the text, and compared with the colours kept from the last read that read them. The key's echo waits for that read. Colours are never read before a key, so a held key reaches the program no later than it would otherwise, and never at other reads, so a stream costs nothing for them. The read program checks each row whole, a row of several colours in 16-cell chunks, and a chunk of several colours cell by cell, 16 cells having measured fastest; on the buffer's last row, which Windows Terminal cuts a cell short, the row is read cell by cell.

### The review cursor

The review position is an anchor: a place in the buffer, a column, and the hash of its line (Dickson, 2026-10-25). Within the last read, each read's alignment maps it: a kept row carries it to the row's new place, so it moves with its text when output scrolls; a rewritten row keeps it, the column clamped to the row's text, and so does a blank row written over in place; a gone row moves it to the nearest surviving row above, or to the caret when nothing survived. A redraw elsewhere (a footer, a spinner, a timer) never moves it. With "review follows caret" on, it follows the caret when a key moved the caret within the key's window, judged from the caret before the keys to the caret after, and when the focus arrives; never when a program moves its cursor while it draws.

Review commands never act on a live provider range. A review command first takes a read, so its alignment maps the anchor onto the text as it is, then moves and speaks within that snapshot. Above the last read, the command reads by place, in one remote operation (`verbatim_uia_rops::terminal_snapshot`), the rows from one screen above the anchor's place to the document's end, and takes the nearest row with its line's text from a screen above the place to a screen below it, the higher first at one distance (Dickson, 2026-10-25): before the history is full, and while nothing is written, the place is exact, a full history moves rows up, and a program that prints its history again longer moves them down. With the read's start refused, the document shrank beneath the rows above the read: an anchor there is found again by its line's text nearest its old place within the read, as after a resize, or else the review position goes to the caret (Dickson, 2026-10-25). When no row there has that text, as when output carried the line further up than one screen while the user reviewed, the review position keeps its place and reads the row there now (Dickson, 2026-10-25). A review line ends at its last character.

Moving by command: when Enter is pressed on a line a key changed since it appeared (so a bare Enter sets no mark and a recalled command does), Verbatim notes the line and sets a mark on the row that holds it once the terminal has answered: the same row in a shell; in Pi's and Claude Code's regular modes, where the input is cleared and printed into the transcript, the new row whose line equals it, looked for until the quiet period passes. A mark is an anchor on its line's first row with the hash of the whole line, moved by the alignment like the review position. Commands entered before Verbatim watched the terminal have no mark; moving past the oldest says nothing more. The exit status is unknown, so there is no success or failure sound.

"Report status bar" (NVDA+End in the desktop layout, NVDA+Shift+End in the laptop layout) reads the footer as it is now, top to bottom: the rows below the caret's row, or, when there are none, the rows at the bottom of the screen a program has rewritten; with neither, the last row with text. It is the way to hear a row that never stops changing.

### Every time limit

- A key's window, and the limit on a typed character's echo: 300 milliseconds after the key reached the program, NVDA's wait for the caret in Windows Terminal. Both are judged at reads, with no timer.
- A row that keeps changing, a row that changed alone before a key, and a lingering row: 2 seconds, which takes in a once-a-second timer's ticks and htop's 1.5-second refresh. Judged at reads, with no timer.
- The quiet period: 1 second, or 1.5 times the time between the row's last two changes when that is longer, so a once-a-second timer is not judged settled between ticks. It is the reading's one timer, armed only while something waits for it, with one wake-up when it ends.
- From key holding (`docs/design/key-holding.md`), unchanged: a held caret key waits at most 150 milliseconds for the read before it; a key pressed behind a held key waits at most 100 milliseconds after that key was passed on; a held key's watch ends with its evidence, at the next key or focus change, or after 10 seconds.

## Limits

Cases no reader can resolve from the text, and cases decided as not handled:

- With a full history, a single read over repeated rows may miss or repeat one line: the last read's rows found by their text can fit where repeated text sits, and the read's rows are placed there. Nothing carries the mistake into the next read (Dickson, 2026-10-25). In a full terminal whose read window of 60 rows holds the same bare prompt, pressing Enter on an empty prompt says nothing.
- After a lost read, "skipped about N lines" counts from where the last read's rows were found by text, which repeated text can put a little off, and a flood of identical lines says "skipped lines" with no number and does not say its newest copies, which look exactly like the screen before (Dickson, 2026-10-25).
- With a full history, rows above the last read's screen that are blank, or repeat the same text, read the same after the buffer moved; with at least one row with text among them, positions are trusted only when that text is in place, so this needs a history block whose rows with text all repeat at the distance the buffer moved.
- A page of a full-screen program that keeps no row of the page before it, its status row included, is a new screen and sets that page aside in place of the shell's screen, so the program closing over a long history is not recognised: the read is lost and says "skipped more than N lines" before the shell's screen (Dickson, 2026-10-25). less, vim, htop and man keep or rewrite a status row, so it needs a program that redraws every row at once.
- A full-screen program already open when Verbatim first reads the terminal, as when Verbatim or the terminal's outpost starts while vim, less or man is open, has no shell's screen set aside: its close over a long history is a lost read, saying the first 30 lines, "skipped more than N lines" and the last 30. A screen starting at the document's start is all the reads show of a program's own screen, and a fresh shell's screen shows the same, so treating such a first read as a set-aside screen would silence a flood on a fresh shell (Dickson, 2026-10-25).
- A write that repeats a row of the aside screen no nearer the document's end than that row was is taken for the aside screen back: a new screen, its lines above the visible screen silent. It is the repeated-text limit's case for the aside screen.
- A program that erases the history and prints it again speaks what changed within the last read's reach and nothing of the rows above it: Pi's Control+O expanding its startup help above the screen is heard as "Tool output: expanded", and the 20 lines it adds above are not heard, as a new screen's rows above its visible screen are not; they are read with the review cursor. The memory holds nothing older to compare them with, and a read of the whole document would speak sixty reprinted lines on every toggle (Dickson, 2026-10-25). Output that repeats the last read's text lower down, its screen's rows included, after the first copy left the buffer, reads exactly as such a history printed again, and the line above the copy is not heard; seen only in a 6-row history (Claude, 2026-10-25, pending Dickson's acceptance).
- "Skipped about N lines" after a clear counts the document's rows, so output that also overflowed the buffer within one read after a clear is undercounted (Dickson, 2026-10-25: floods need no exact count).
- The review position above the last read, whose line output carried further up than a screen while the user reviewed, keeps its place and reads the newer line there now (Dickson, 2026-10-25).
- A count the keys change that only shrinks, such as fzf's "12/340" becoming "2/340", is silent, because a row that only lost text is silent everywhere; that same rule keeps a list's old selected row silent when its marker moves, which no rule could tell from a shrinking count without a special case (Dickson, 2026-10-24).
- A blank row a line feed wrote and one the cursor jumped over look the same; the blank-lines rule judges by where the cursor rests.
- Output written and erased between two reads is never seen.
- A line read half written (a word split across the first two reads of a streamed line, or a line written in two chunks) is spoken in two pieces.
- Output written in the same read as a resize is not heard.
- Lines that scrolled out of a scroll region between reads are seen only if the terminal kept them in its history.
- Programs that wrap their own text with line breaks (Pi) give separate lines, spoken in pieces.
- A program that clears the screen and reprints exactly the earlier screen is silent (Dickson, 2026-10-24); output repeated after a clear, when it leaves the rows as moving or erasing rows would, is silent too.
- Text erased and written back within 2 seconds is silent, wherever it comes back on the screen, so a wrapped line's last row printed again as a line of its own in that time is silent; it is text heard before.
- What an overflow pushes out while held unspoken, an unfinished word or a volatile row's latest value, is not heard (Dickson, 2026-10-24).
- `cls` typed on a prompt on the screen's first row, as after an earlier `cls`, with no read between Windows Terminal's homing of the caret and its erase, reads exactly as Control+W erasing the last word: the row is the caret's line, which only lost text, and the prompt is not said (Dickson, 2026-10-25).
- Windows Terminal's clear is not taken for a new screen when the last read had a row with exactly the text the clear leaves, such as a bare prompt from an earlier Enter on an empty line: that row is kept, and the prompt is not spoken.
- A screen that shows no text is taken for a program between its clear and its reprint, so a screen a program really left blank is not noticed until text appears on it.
- Rows a program reprints that no read ever showed, such as Pi's earlier conversation when Verbatim began watching mid-way, are spoken as new.
- Colours that change with no key and no text change are not heard (Dickson, 2026-10-23), so Pi's tool state is heard only through its text.
- A hidden cursor parked away from the user's attention gives the caret nothing useful to say.
- Panes drawn side by side by one program share rows, so a change is not attributed to its pane (Dickson, 2026-10-23).
- Windows Terminal raises no text-changed event for writes made only of blank lines, so such a scroll is seen at the next read.

## Costs

Measured on 2026-10-23 and 2026-10-24, 120 columns by 30 rows, both terminals, unless stated:

- A read of the screen's text alone, as one remote operation: 0.19 milliseconds in the console host, 0.27 in Windows Terminal. A 30-row read 0.12 milliseconds; 1,000 rows 1.3 to 3.4; a full history of 9,001 rows 9.4 to 29 (the snapshot alone 25 to 26 milliseconds in the console host through the outpost, 1.1 million UTF-16 units, 31 to 38 milliseconds for the whole read). The terminal's writer waits while a read copies.
- Each read takes one screen of rows more than the screen: about 0.1 milliseconds more, 0.45 to 1.06 milliseconds against 0.37 to 0.75 in the console host (2026-10-24). The positions test compares one screen of hashes, microseconds.
- A whole read only for a lost read with a full history: 25 to 38 milliseconds of snapshot, during which the terminal's writer waits, at most once per group of spoken output, never with output off (accepted by Dickson on 2026-10-24); and about 9 milliseconds of alignment of 9,000 rows, which runs on Core's reducer once the reading moves there, measured then and moved off the reducer as an effect if over its budget, with 1.1 MB of text crossing the pipe once per such read.
- A full-screen program closing: the read after it starts at the range kept at the alternate screen's top, which the main screen takes as its first row, so it reads the whole document. In a full history that is 1.1 million UTF-16 units, the text's `GetText` alone 28.6 to 30.5 milliseconds in the console host and 11.3 to 11.6 in Windows Terminal, against 0.44 to 1.29 for two screens (measured 2026-10-25), once per exit, the terminal's writer waiting. It is kept as it is (Dickson, 2026-10-25): starting such a read two screens above the document's end would also start there after a fresh screen, where the same kept start must read from the document's start to count its output exactly.
- The aside screen: at most 60 hashes and distances, about a kilobyte, while one is held; the check runs only on a read that keeps nothing of the last read, one hash lookup for each row of that read, or of the whole document a lost read already holds. No call, wake-up or timer.
- A review command above the last read: one more remote operation, from a screen above its place to the document's end; searching a screen below the place as well as above adds no call and no row to it, only up to a screen more of hash comparisons (`docs/performance.md`, "A terminal review command above the last read").
- A refused start that keeps nothing and finds no aside screen: one whole-document read, bounded by the shrunken document, as any lost read; `clear; dir` reads about a hundred rows. The alignment's vote over the screen rows' hashes and the history block's admitted shift add no call, wake-up or timer, and the vote is taken only when a row of the history block has its text lower than its place, so a stream takes none (`docs/performance.md`, "The terminal memory's cost, estimated").
- A terminal's first read, when the document goes on above its screen: one more remote operation of two screens of rows, about 0.45 to 1.06 milliseconds in the console host, once each time the outpost begins reading a terminal (the focus arriving at it).
- Colours, in the first read after a key only, added to the time from that key to its speech: a plain shell screen 2.6 milliseconds more in the console host and 1.6 in Windows Terminal; PSReadLine's line 1.9 and 1.2; Claude Code's screen 1.0 and 0.7; Pi's screen 8.0 and 4.9; the worst case, every cell a different colour, 72 and 31 (six programs, none over 4,200 instructions). Read with separate calls instead, the runs of a colourful screen cost 56 and 118 milliseconds; read with every read, colours would cost 6 to 12 percent of a core while Pi or Claude Code streams.
- Memory: the last read's rows and the aside screen, a few kilobytes per terminal, however long it ran and however it was flooded.
- The quiet-period timer: one wake-up for each thing that waits for it, when it ends.
- The terminals' own cost of being watched, unchanged by any design: the console host raises about 13,600 events in a 10,000-line flood, about 40 microseconds each to a listening client; with Verbatim a console-host flood took 2.9 to 3.7 seconds against 1.6 to 1.9 with no screen reader, and a Windows Terminal flood 1.5 to 1.7 seconds against 1.4 to 1.6 (2026-10-07, debug build).

The budget for the build: on the typing path no rise but the colours of the read after each key; for output, no rise in wake-ups beyond the quiet-period timer; processor time, wake-ups and change-to-speech time measured against the baseline taken before the redesign, for typing, a 20,000-line flood, a Pi stream at its recorded pace, a clock ticking once a second, and a full-screen program refreshing rows (`measure_` scenarios, `docs/performance.md`).

## Testing

- The simulated terminal (`verbatim-term-sim`, in tests only): an emulator on a VT parser with its own grid, every row carrying a hidden identity through scrolls, the full history's movement and clears, adapters that answer as the two terminals' providers do, and a virtual clock. It is checked against the real terminals by `cargo xtask regenerate-terminal-conformance-expectations`, run on demand on this machine or in the VM (it opens real windows), which writes each stream of the corpus into both terminals chunk by chunk and saves what they answered; a unit test replays the streams in the simulator and compares. Nothing in this design changes those expectations: they record the terminals, not the reader.
- The oracle judges and never drives a rule. It checks invariants over the simulator's ground truth for the corpus, random streams with random read points, and every stream of up to four operations on a tiny terminal:
  - Every silence is accounted for, judged by text (Dickson, 2026-10-23): every line whose text no earlier read showed, on either screen, while the terminal had the focus and output was reported, is reported (a line, its growth, a rewritten row's change, or a flood's summary) or withheld with a reason the reader states.
  - A reason is accepted when any explanation consistent with what the reads showed makes it true (principle 5): a volatile row changed at an earlier read and changes again under some consistent pairing of rows; an unfinished word is the end of a line that grows; a row that only lost text shows a part of what an earlier read showed there; a spinner's change replaces one symbol. The oracle never consults the hidden identity of the row a program wrote to, which no read reveals.
  - A held line is reported, or its text replaced while unreported, before the reader is asked once more after the stream with time passed.
  - Every spoken line is text the stream printed, or a summary; a line is reported twice only as a fault when its text stayed on the screen between.
  - For every row whose text is unique in both reads and on the screen in both, the alignment maps it where that text went; with no key pressed, following off and no switch of screens, the review position stays on its text while it is unique on the screen; padding is never spoken.
  A failure the oracle finds is one of three things (Dickson, 2026-10-24). When two explanations fit everything the reads showed, the consistent-explanation rule above already accepts it. When it is a case listed under "Limits", the oracle accepts it only through that limit's own named reason, whose precondition it checks against the ground truth, so a limit never covers a different fault; each limit has its smallest stream as a fixed test. Otherwise it is a defect in the code, fixed in the code. A failure from random or tiny streams is never the reason for a new rule; a rule needs the same failure in a real program, recording or scenario. The recordings of real programs pass with no violation and no limit's reason, but for `pi-changed-above`, which needs the limit for a rewritten history (Dickson, 2026-10-25). Adding a limit is a change to this design, reviewed like any other. There are no pinned cases numbered by random case.
- The corpus and the tiny streams run with histories that are not full, so they show no violation and no limit, but for `pi-changed-above`, whose program erases its history and prints it again and which passes through the limit for a rewritten history alone (Dickson, 2026-10-25). The random streams use a 12 by 5 terminal with 6 rows of history, full most of the time, and three limits, each with its precondition checked against the ground truth:
  - Repeated text with a full history: a missed, repeated or misplaced line is accepted only when the buffer moved since the last read and the last read's rows with text fit the read equally well at more than one place, or the line's text is not unique in the main buffer, its history included (Claude, 2026-10-25, pending Dickson's acceptance).
  - A lost read: new lines above the visible screen that a reader counts rather than speaks are accepted only when the buffer moved since the last read by more than the read covered.
  - A rewritten history: a missed line above the read's first row is accepted only when rows were erased from the buffer's front with the history since the last read, or a missed line above a row the reader took for a row of the last read that left the buffer since, that row a copy of its text, as output repeating the last read's text lower down reads exactly as a history printed again (the second case Claude's, 2026-10-25, pending Dickson's acceptance). The reader states it when its start was refused with something kept, or, found by text, the last read's rows went lower than their places. Over the 512 random streams it is needed by 1, the repeated-text precondition by 2, the lost read's by none and the read with no text's by 35; of the corpus, only `pi-changed-above` needs a limit, this one, in both terminals; the tiny streams need none (2026-10-25).
  - A read with no text, which every stream may show: the next read is judged against the read before it, and a held row that only reads with no text follow is left to the reader's timer, which the oracle's reads do not run.
- The oracle does not check exact counts; Core's tests and the scenarios do.
- What rewritten rows, keys, lists and colours say is decided speech, not a property of the stream, so it is tested by unit tests that state a stream and the exact speech, one or a few per rule, never by the oracle (`docs/testing.md`). An expectation changes only with an NVDA capture or a decision recorded here, and a commit that changes one says which.

Which tests cover which rules, by their names today (the reader's tests in `crates/verbatim-text/src/terminal_reading/tests.rs`, the memory's in `crates/verbatim-text/src/terminal_rows/tests.rs`, Core's in `crates/verbatim-core/tests/terminal.rs`, the outpost's in `crates/verbatim-outpost/src/terminal/tests.rs`), and the scenarios, are listed in each test file's module documentation, one or more tests a rule. Scenarios by rule: when and what to read, `*_flood`, `*_history_flood_during_group`, `*_control_flood`; positions and the alignment, `*_footer_flood`, `*_typing` (`cls`), `conhost_wrapped_flood`; a lost read, `*_full_history_flood`, `*_scrollback_overflow`, `*_scrollback_overflow_during_group`, `conhost_footer_overflow`, `conhost_fresh_flood`, `windows_terminal_clear_flood`, `windows_terminal_cleared_flood`; screens and the aside screen, `*_full_screen`, `*_pager`, `*_pager_history`; new text, `*_commands`, `*_long_lines`, `*_short_output`, `*_leave_flood`, `*_two_windows`, `windows_terminal_tabs`, `windows_terminal_close_tab`; changed rows, `*_progress`, `*_redraw_limit`; keys and typing, `*_typing`, `*_up_typing`, `*_line_key_flood`, `*_editing`, `*_spoken_password`; lists, `*_marker_list`, `*_redrawn_list`; the review cursor, `*_review_output`, `*_review_grid`, `*_review_padding`; floods, `*_same_flood`, `*_history_flood`, `*_raised_flood`, `*_shift_flood`.

Recordings and scenarios to add, each tied to the rule it gives a real case for: Pi's regular mode resized, set aside until the corpus can record a resize (Dickson, 2026-10-25), its change above the screen recorded as `pi-changed-above`; a cargo build, whose bar sits below its output (volatile rows during a flood), recorded as the corpus's `cargo` and its scenario still to add; fzf (held for keys, a key's answer), waiting until Dickson installs it; Claude Code streaming a reply (growing lines, lingering), recorded as `claude-code-reply`; htop is dropped (Dickson, 2026-10-25); and the end-to-end scenarios the old design listed and that are still missing: the landing rule's keys; Control+W, Control+U and Control+K; tab completion; suggestions; a completion list; menu completion; history search; output while typing; pasting; slow echo; a spinner with a message; several progress lines; resizing; a list shown by colour; a checkbox list; a refreshing full-screen script; fzf-like filtering; the review cursor kept through a footer redraw and moved with its text through a scroll; output just after the focus with an interactive shell; the console host's real password prompt; Pi in both modes; and, as decided on 2026-10-24, Verbatim's own streaming program (`verbatim-stream-test`) at up to 60 frames a second in each way programs stream, with a footer, a header and a middle status row, expected to speak the streamed text once, in order, with no word split, whatever the reads' timing.

## Order of work

Each part is one step in the sense of `REVIEW.md`: built with its tests and documents, its related scenarios run, then reviewed once in full. Nothing is pushed until the last. The memory and alignment are in `crates/verbatim-text/src/terminal_rows.rs` and `terminal_rows/align.rs`, the rules in `crates/verbatim-text/src/terminal_reading.rs`, both run by the outpost (`crates/verbatim-outpost/src/terminal.rs`), and the flood policy in `crates/verbatim-core/src/terminal.rs`.

1. The model of 2026-10-25 in the pure crate: the remembered history, the rotation, its candidates, the first-row tie-break, the history match of whole reads, set-aside screens and the overflow rules removed; positions, the alignment by text when they are not trusted, the lost read with its whole read, new screens and the aside screen as above; "about" and "skipped lines" handed to Core's flood policy; the review anchor's search above the last read; the oracle's three limits. Each rule with its unit test; the recordings and the oracle run.
2. The move into Core, as decided: the memory, the alignment and the rules in the reducer, the outpost reading what Core asks; keys reaching the rules through Core, with the hook's separate report to the outpost and all that carries it removed; a read's speech counted after every key whose window holds it, so a deferred key does not cut a held key's answer; one caret, so a console-host caret move with no text reaches the rules. The whole document's transfer and the reducer's time for its alignment measured, and moved off the reducer as an effect if over budget.
3. "Report status bar"; then moving by command, with their scenarios in both terminals, in a shell and in Pi's regular mode.
4. The recordings listed under "Testing" and their conformance expectations, after checking that the NVDA pin equals the installed build for any capture.
5. End-to-end scenarios: the existing terminal scenarios brought back to passing, then the ones listed under "Testing", then the streaming program's.
6. Measurements after, against the baseline, recorded in `docs/performance.md`.
7. Documents: `docs/parity/terminals.md`, the crates' documents and the roadmap's M4 entries follow this design.
8. The whole terminal suite, `cargo xtask ci`, and the push.

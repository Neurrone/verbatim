# verbatim-term-sim

A simulated terminal for Verbatim's tests (`docs/design/terminal-model.md`, section 9): a VT emulator whose rows carry identities, answering the questions Verbatim's read program asks of a terminal's UI Automation provider as the console host or Windows Terminal answers them. Its answers are checked against the real terminals by the conformance expectations in its `conformance` folder. It is used by tests only, and it depends on no Windows bindings, so the platform-neutral crates' tests can use it; `cargo xtask ci`'s check of the platform-neutral crates does not cover it, since it is no product code.

## Public API

- `Terminal::new(flavor, width, height, history)` makes an empty terminal of a `Flavor` (`ConsoleHost` or `WindowsTerminal`), `width` cells by `height` rows, its history holding up to `history` rows above the screen. `Terminal::write(bytes)` writes to it as a program writes to its output.
- `Terminal::snapshot(&Query)` answers one read as the read program takes it (`verbatim_uia_rops::terminal_snapshot`), returning a `Snapshot`: the text from the read's start to the document's end, the screen's text, whether the start was used or refused, whether the read and the screen start at the document's start, the document's first row, the caret's row, its column, how many lines the caret moves to the document's end, and where the next read starts. A `Query` gives the start the last read named (`Range`), the screen's height as last found, and whether to read the whole text. A call the real provider would fail returns `Refused`.
- `Terminal::colours()` gives each screen row's stretches of one foreground and background as shown (`Run`), from the screen's top to the document's last row. `Terminal::text(range)` gives a range's text.
- `Terminal::grid()` exposes the state: the main and alternate `Buffer`s, their `Row`s with their `RowId`s, cells and styles, and the cursor. Row identities follow rows through scrolls, rotation and clears, which no real terminal exposes; the oracle's checks rest on them.
- `Terminal::printed()` is the oracle's ground truth: every line the stream has printed and ended with a line feed so far, in the order they were finished (`PrintedLine`: the text, the rows' identities, the write that finished the line, the write in which it first changed and the write in which one of its rows left the buffer, if either happened, and whether it repeats the screen the last clear erased). `Terminal::writes()` counts the writes. `Grid::screen_lines()` gives the lines with text on the screen shown.
- `oracle`: the invariants of the design's section 9 and what they are checked over. A reader under test implements `Reader`: it gives each read's `Query`, is handed the read's `Snapshot`, and answers with `Heard` (what it would say, as `Spoken` reports that each name the row their line starts on, and optionally where it thinks each row of its previous read went and which row of the read its review position is on) or asks to read again at once (`Reply::Again`). `observe` reads a terminal once before anything is written, then writes a stream chunk by chunk and reads after each, recording each read's rows as they truly are (`Observation`, `Seen`), with the lines on the screen shown and the main buffer's row identities at that moment; `check` (or `Run::violations`) lists every `Violation`. `Op` is one of the operations of section 3.2 as bytes, `stream` turns operations and read points into a corpus-shaped `Stream`, `every_stream` lists every stream of up to a given number of operations of an alphabet (`TINY_ALPHABET`), and `Size::SMALL` and `Size::TINY` are the random and exhaustive cases' terminals, with `WORDS` the random streams' words.
- `screen_rows(screen)` counts the rows of a screen's text as Verbatim counts them.
- `conformance`: the corpus (`streams()`, each a `Stream` of chunks), the expectations (`Expectation`, `Answer`, `expectation_path`), `simulate` and `replay`, which reads the simulated terminal after each chunk as the runner reads a real one and lists where it differs from an expectation, and `history(flavor)`, the history each terminal keeps by default.

## How it works

The grid (`grid.rs`) is a buffer of rows with fixed coordinates. The main buffer holds up to its capacity, the screen at its bottom; a line feed on the screen's last row moves the screen down the buffer until the buffer is full, then discards the first row so that every row's content moves up one coordinate, as the real buffers rotate. The alternate buffer is one screen tall. A scroll region whose top is the screen's top scrolls into the history as the whole screen does, the rows below it keeping their places and the new blank row coming in at the region's bottom; any other region scrolls among its own rows. Erasing the screen on the main buffer pushes its rows with text into the history and then erases every cell left on it, rows of colour without text included; erasing the history drops the rows above the screen. Erasing, inserted characters, the cells deleting characters shifts in at a row's end, rows scrolled in and the alternate screen when it is entered take the current colours, without bold, faint or reverse video; erasing never ends a wrapped line. The console host marks a row as wrapping as soon as its last column is written, and an explicit line feed, index or next line on it clears the mark, so a full row followed by anything but a line feed joins the next row in the text; a row inserted at a marked row takes the mark. Windows Terminal marks a row only when a character wraps onto the next.

The VT parser is the `vte` crate; `vt.rs` maps what it parses onto the grid: printing with deferred wrapping, which reverse index, inserting and deleting rows and cursor moves cancel; carriage return, line feed, backspace and tab; cursor moves, absolute and relative: moving up from at or below the scroll region's top stops at the top margin and moving down from at or above its bottom stops at the bottom margin, wherever the move starts, while the vertical position relative to the cursor (VPR) stops only at the screen's edge; saving and restoring the cursor, one slot for each screen, the alternate screen's kept between its visits; erasing in the line and the display; inserting and deleting rows and characters; scrolling; scroll regions, margins out of order ignored; the alternate screen (mode 1049; modes 47 and 1047 switch no screen in either terminal, which ignore them); the whole screen in reverse video (mode 5, a visual bell's flash); the cursor's visibility (mode 25); and colours (the sixteen-colour palette as Campbell's, bold shown as the bright version of the default foreground or of a basic colour chosen with SGR 30 to 37, but not of a 256-colour index or true colour of the same value, faint shown by halving each channel of the foreground, 256 colours, true colour, and reverse video). Anything else is ignored: wide characters, which take two cells, autowrap turned off (mode 7), and the colon form of true colour are not modelled.

The ground truth (`truth.rs`) is recorded by the grid as the stream is written. A line is a row and the rows after it while each wraps onto the next. A line is recorded as printed each time an explicit line feed (line feed, vertical tab, form feed, index or next line) on its last row moves the cursor off it on the main screen, it has text other than spaces, its first row did not continue a row that had already left the buffer, and it is not the line recorded last on the same rows, unchanged since. How its characters came to be does not matter, so a line redrawn after a carriage return is recorded as the line feed finds it, and every redraw ended by a line feed is recorded again. A line changes, and records the write it changed in, when a character of its rows changes (written over, erased, or shifted by characters inserted or deleted before it), when a row of it starts or stops wrapping onto the next, or when the row above it starts or stops wrapping onto it, which scrolling a region beneath a wrapping row also does. Moving changes nothing: a line scrolled into the history or pushed there by erasing the screen is the same line. A row leaving the buffer (rotation out of a full history, erasing the history, deletion, or scrolling out of a region) is not a change; the line records the write it left in. Erasing the screen on the main buffer records the lines with text the screen showed just before; the lines printed next are a reprint while each repeats the next of those, from the first, in order, and the first that differs, or one printed after all were repeated, ends the reprint.

The oracle (`oracle.rs`) decides from that truth and the reads which printed lines are new output, and checks five invariants:

- Every line of new output is reported exactly once, as a line, a redrawn row's speech or in a summary, and in order (`Missed`, `Repeated`, `OutOfOrder`). A printed line is found by the first read taken on the main screen at or after the write that finished it, before the write it first changed in and before the write one of its rows left the buffer in, whether or not that read's text covers it. It is new output when a read found it, it is not a reprint, and its text is not that of any line on the screen of the read before (the first read, taken before anything is written, has none before it); from then on it is required, whatever happens to it later. So a footer, input box or status line drawn again with text the screen already showed is not required, and output written over the rows that held one is. The exemptions follow from when a line is found: a line changed in the write that finished it, or before the next read, was written and erased or rewritten between two reads and is never found; so is a line one of whose rows left a full history or a scroll region in that time, and one finished while the alternate screen was up at every read until it changed or left. The order is the order reads found the lines, top to bottom in the main buffer among the lines one read found, since a reader sees positions, not the order of writing. A line reported while it was still being written counts from that report when the line it became only grew from what the read showed, and what was reported of it with its growth must add up to its text (`Incomplete`). Resizing is not modelled, so the design's exemption for lines written in the same read as a resize does not arise.
- Everything reported is text the read shows: a line exactly the line at the row it names, growth and a redrawn row's speech part of that line, and every report and every row a summary counts naming the first row of a line of the read (`Misquoted`, `NotALine`).
- For every row with text unchanged between two reads and on no other row of either, a reader that reports where rows went maps it to the row it really became (`Mismapped`).
- Padding is never reported: a report that matches the read only with the spaces after a line's end is `Padded`.
- A reader that reports a review position keeps it on the same row: when the row it was on after one read is among the rows of the next and both reads are on the same screen, it is there (`ReviewMoved`), and it is always a row of the read (`ReviewOutside`). The streams press no keys and nothing follows the caret, so the design's conditions for this invariant always hold; a row that left the buffer, or that the next read does not cover, is not checked, and neither is a reader that reports no review position.

Reports of lines that are not new output, such as a footer drawn again or a redrawn row's change, are allowed by these invariants; what they say is decided speech, tested by unit tests of the reading core.

The provider (`provider.rs`) answers as the real providers do, each rule measured by the conformance expectations:

- Positions are a row's coordinate and a column; a position anywhere in the allocated buffer is accepted, unwritten rows reading as blank, and one past it is refused, so a range kept on the main screen reads the alternate screen while its row is within the alternate screen's height.
- The document ends at the end of the lower of the last row with text and the cursor's row. In the console host a row marked as wrapping counts as a row with text, so a whole row of spaces written without a line feed, or such a row erased, keeps the document from ending above it.
- The buffer's last cell reads in the colours the next character would be written in, whatever it holds: the alternate screen's last cell, and the main screen's once the history is full.
- Rows are padded to the width and joined by line breaks, none between a row and the row its line wraps onto.
- The console host's ranges end before a row's line break, so an expanded line, the screen and the document end without one. Windows Terminal's end after it, except on the buffer's last row, which has no row after it for the break: there the range ends at the width, but its text leaves out the row's last cell. That is the alternate screen's last row, and the main screen's once the history is full.
- Moving by lines stops at each row's start and at the document's end, so the last step forward goes from the last row's start to its end.
- Expanding a position past the document's last row gives that row.

## Conformance

`cargo xtask regenerate-terminal-conformance-expectations [--stream <name>]... [--flavor console-host|windows-terminal]...` (`xtask/src/conformance.rs`) writes each stream of `conformance/streams` into the real console host and the end-to-end suite's pinned Windows Terminal, 120 by 30. It reads each after every chunk with `verbatim_uia_rops::terminal_snapshot`, the caret classically, and every screen cell's colours, and writes `conformance/expected/<stream>.<flavor>.json`. It opens terminal windows, so it runs on demand on a desktop, never in continuous integration: when a stream is added, when the simulator's behaviour is changed, and when Windows or Windows Terminal updates. The changed expectation files are reviewed in their commit.

The terminal runs the same executable's `conformance-player`, which connects back over a local socket, writes each chunk it is sent to its console, and then sets the window's title to a token naming the chunk. A terminal changes its title only once it has parsed everything written before, and Windows Terminal parses it itself, where a cursor-position query could be answered by the pseudoconsole in front of it; so the runner waits for the token in the title before it reads. The console host is started through `cmd /c start`, since given any standard handles it serves them as a pseudoconsole instead of opening a window; the player opens `CONOUT$` itself for the same reason, and sets the console mode programs run with (processed output, wrapping at the end of a line, VT processing, and a line feed that does not return the carriage, which is why the simulator's line feed never does).

Colours are read cell by cell: over a stretch of two colours both providers answer 0 rather than "mixed", and their format unit does not stop at every change of colour. A read that falls back from the remote program to the classic one fails the run rather than being recorded.

The expectations depend on the terminals' settings on the machine that recorded them: the default history (9,001 rows in all in the console host, 9,001 above the screen in Windows Terminal), the Campbell colour scheme and the 120 by 30 size.

`cargo xtask record-terminal-streams [--recipe <name>]...` (`xtask/src/record.rs`) adds recorded programs to the corpus. Each recipe in `conformance/recipes` names a command and the input to type into it; the program runs in a pseudoconsole of 120 by 30, and what it writes is saved with when it came (`Stream::at_ms`), split into chunks where the output paused for 30 milliseconds or input was typed. The pseudoconsole's own requests to its terminal, programs' questions to the terminal (whose answers would arrive in the player's input) and title changes are left out. The recordings are bash with readline from Git for Windows, vim, less, Windows PowerShell with PSReadLine 2.0, Claude Code (an Ink program; nothing is sent), Pi in its full-screen and regular modes, and a script drawing progress the ways programs do.

## Tests

- `src/tests.rs`: the emulator's own behaviour, one test per operation: padding and the document's end, wrapping, rotation under fixed coordinates, row identity through a scroll, a kept range on the alternate screen, a kept start, reverse video, and Windows Terminal's line breaks.
- `src/tests.rs` also pins the ground truth, one test for each of the grid's hooks, so that removing any of them fails a test: a line finished and one still being written, a wrapped line over its rows, a line redrawn after a carriage return, a second line feed on an unchanged line, a line changed by a character written over it, by erasing, by a character inserted before its text and by one deleted, a row starting to wrap, a row scrolled under a wrapping row, a line whose first row continued a row that left, the write a line left a full history in, the history erased and a region scrolled down, a reprint after a clear ended by a line that differs, and a line on the alternate screen.
- `tests/oracle.rs`: the oracle against readers whose faults are known, each asserted with its exact violations. A reader that speaks every row differing from the same place in the previous read, with its review position on the read's last row, is caught on a scroll repeating the lines it moved, mismapping its rows and moving its review position, and on a flood missing the lines pushed into the history. A reader that diffs the lines of whole texts by a longest common subsequence, reading from the kept start as the read program does, is caught on the flood missing the lines rotated above its kept start. A silent reader is caught on the footer stream missing only the new line, not the line the footer showed first; on the corpus's Ink stream missing the first frame, the line streamed over the frame's rows and the frame drawn after a read found it erased, where the first definition, which required only lines printed onto blank cells, let it pass; and after a clear that a read found, missing only the line before it, not its reprint. A reader whose summary counts a wrapped line's second row and a row past the read is caught naming them. A reader that reports from the ground truth passes the fixed streams, 512 random streams of 1 to 39 operations with random read points on `Size::SMALL` in either terminal (`proptest`, configured entirely in the test, with a fixed seed, no failure files and nothing taken from the environment, so every run checks the same streams), every stream of up to four operations of `TINY_ALPHABET` on `Size::TINY` in both terminals, and the conformance corpus. These tests run in continuous integration; in a debug build on Dickson's x64 machine, `cargo test -p verbatim-term-sim --test oracle` runs its tests in about 3.4 seconds once built, 3.7 seconds for the whole command.
- `tests/conformance.rs`: every stream of the corpus replayed and compared field by field with both terminals' expectations, failing with each differing field's first difference. The corpus holds hand-written streams, one per kind of operation; streams that pin rules found in review (a pending wrap cancelled, cursor moves inside margins and from outside them, the saved cursor across screens, erasing in a background colour, deleting characters and entering the alternate screen in one, modes 47 and 1047, erasing while bold, wrap marks through erasing and through index and next line, bold over each kind of colour, faint, Windows Terminal's last cell, the colours of the buffer's last cell, and rows of spaces at the document's end); and the recordings.

The read program's own behaviour, both paths, is tested in `mockapp` (`crates/mockapp/tests/terminal_snapshot.rs`).
